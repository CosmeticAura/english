Elements and plot

Plot diagram

Exposition

The Das family, Indian Americans from New Jersey, tour Orissa with Mr. Kapasi, their 46 year old guide, on the way to the Sun Temple at Konarak. Mr. and Mrs. Das bicker and act more like siblings than parents.

Inciting incident

Mr. Kapasi says his other job is interpreting for a doctor. Mrs. Das calls it "But so romantic," and his fascination with her begins.

Rising action

He tells her more patient stories, eats lunch with the family, writes his address for her and imagines years of letters. At the Sun Temple he wants to be alone with her, then stretches the trip to the hills at Udayagiri and Khandagiri.

Complication

Mrs. Das stays in the car and confesses that Bobby is not Raj's son. She wants Mr. Kapasi to "interpret" her pain and make her feel better.

Climax

He asks, "Is it really pain you feel, Mrs. Das, or is it guilt?" She glares and walks off, and he realizes he was "not even important enough to be properly insulted." His fantasy ends here.

Reversal

Her spilled puffed rice draws the monkeys after her, and the story turns from Mr. Kapasi's private hopes to the family's danger.

Falling action

The monkeys surround Bobby and hit him with the stick. Mr. Kapasi drives them off with a branch and carries Bobby back.

Resolution

The family patches Bobby up and decides to go back to the hotel. "Poor Bobby," Mrs. Das says.

Denouement

When she pulls out her hairbrush, the slip with Mr. Kapasi's address blows away. Only he sees it, "knowing that this was the picture of the Das family he would preserve forever in his mind."

Easy way to remember: everything up the slope is his hope growing. The peak is the guilt question. Everything down the slope is that hope falling apart.

Literary elements

Character

ProtagonistMr. Kapasi. Antagonistno villain; the closest is Mrs. Das, or his own fantasy.

Dynamic, roundMr. Kapasi goes from flattered and hopeful to crushed and clear eyed. StaticMr. and Mrs. Das end exactly as careless as they started. FlatRonny, Tina, and mostly Mr. Das.

FoilMr. Das (only sees through his camera and tour book) against Mr. Kapasi (watches people closely). Mrs. Das also mirrors Mr. Kapasi's wife: two unhappy marriages.

Five ways the author shows character

Dialogue: Mrs. Das snaps "Leave me alone" at her own daughter Tina.

Action: she argues about taking Tina to the restroom, then doesn't hold her hand, and eats her puffed rice without offering anyone some.

Thoughts and feelings: Mr. Kapasi's daydream of letters "between nations."

What others say: Mrs. Das calls his job "a big responsibility," while his wife calls him a "doctor's assistant."

Narrator's description: her big sunglasses, the strawberry on her blouse, her painted nails.

Conflict

Internal: Mr. Kapasi's loneliness and failed dreams; Mrs. Das's eight years of guilt.

External: Mr. and Mrs. Das bickering about the car and the bathroom; Mr. Kapasi against Mrs. Das at the climax; Bobby against the monkeys.

Diction

"Interpreter" denotes someone who translates languages, but it connotes someone who understands people's pain. That is what Mrs. Das wants, and Mr. Kapasi can't do it. "Romantic" just means interesting to her, but it connotes love to him.

Point of view

Third person limited. We only get Mr. Kapasi's thoughts, so we share his fantasy and feel the drop when it breaks. We never really know what Mrs. Das thinks.

Setting

A hot, dry Saturday in July in Orissa, India: the car, a roadside restaurant, the Sun Temple at Konarak, and the hills of Udayagiri and Khandagiri. The family are tourists in their parents' home country.

Theme

People can share a language and still fail to understand each other. Also: loneliness inside a marriage, fantasy against reality, and feeling foreign in your own heritage.

Tone

Quiet, detached and ironic, with a sad edge. The narrator never judges the characters out loud; the details do it.

Techniques in this story

Irony

Situational: Mr. Kapasi is a professional interpreter, yet he completely misreads Mrs. Das, and the person who wants an "interpreter" faces no language barrier at all. Dramatic: we know Bobby isn't Raj's son, but Mr. Das has no idea.

Symbolism

Address slip blowing away = his hope of a connection gone. Puffed rice = Mrs. Das's carelessness, which literally leads the monkeys to her son. Camera and tour book = Mr. Das sees life through a lens instead of looking. Sunglasses = Mrs. Das hiding from everyone. The tired setting sun Surya = Mr. Kapasi's day and hope winding down.

Foreshadowing

Mr. Kapasi warns the monkeys are "more hungry than dangerous" and not to provoke them with food. Bobby hands a monkey a stick, the same stick later used on him.

Motif

Monkeys, photographs and pictures, and puffed rice keep coming back.

Juxtaposition

The temple's carvings of lovers next to two loveless marriages. Mr. Kapasi's dream of letters next to Mrs. Das's confession, which shows she never saw him that way.

Simile and imagery

Mr. and Mrs. Das "behaved like an older brother and sister." The monkeys' tails "dangled like a series of ropes." Tina's doll's hair looks chopped "with a pair of dull scissors."

Allusion

Mythological: Surya, the sun god, and the hanuman monkeys (named for the monkey god Hanuman). Pop culture: the TV show Dallas.

Mood

Stuffy and awkward in the car, uneasy at the temple, tense and frightening when Bobby is attacked.

Paradox

The title itself: an interpreter of maladies who can't interpret the one person who asks him to.

The rest of the handout list (personification, anaphora, onomatopoeia, rhyme and so on) doesn't really show up in this story, so don't force it.

Class packet: guiding question answers

Exposition, pp. 43 to 46

What do the Dases fight about? Who has to take Tina to the restroom. Mrs. Das gives in, but she doesn't hold Tina's hand. That shows she's a distant mother.

How do they look? Young, Indian, but dressed like foreigners. Mr. Kapasi thinks they act more like siblings than a married couple.

Why are they in India? Both parents were born in America. Their own parents retired to India, so the family visits every couple of years. They're tourists here.

Mr. Das calling his wife "Mina" to Tina feels strange to Mr. Kapasi. In India parents wouldn't do that. It shows how American, and how unlike parents, they are.

Their regular life: they live in New Jersey. Mr. Das teaches science. Mrs. Das is home with the kids and bored.

Characterize Mrs. Das: bored, self absorbed, distant from her kids and husband, hiding behind her sunglasses.

Rising action, pp. 47 to 52

The Bombay film magazine in English: she's caught between two cultures, Indian on the outside but reading about India like a foreigner. It's also her way of escaping her life.

Not stopping Tina playing with the door locks: she doesn't pay attention to her kids.

Mr. Das photographing the hanumans and the starving man with his bullocks: he sees India as a tourist through his camera, as scenery, not as people.

Mrs. Das painting her nails: she shuts everyone out and focuses on herself.

Dallas: an American TV soap opera about a rich, dramatic Texas family. Mr. Kapasi learned what he knows about America from it. The real Das family is ordinary and childish, not glamorous.

Narrative perspective: third person limited. We only know Mr. Kapasi's thoughts, which is why the narrator knows so much about him.

His other job: interpreting for a doctor, putting patients' symptoms into words. Interpreting is spoken and explains meaning; translating is turning written words into another language.

"Romantic" and "neat": Mrs. Das finds his job interesting. He hesitates because he's never thought of it as anything special.

Malady = an illness. Assiduous = careful and hard working.

Why he doesn't like his job: he took it to pay a doctor's bills for his son, who later died. It reminds him of that and of his failed dream of being a scholar of languages.

Why Mrs. Kapasi disapproves: the job reminds her of the son they lost. She calls him a "doctor's assistant".

Rising action, pp. 52 to 61

Mrs. Kapasi vs Mrs. Das: Has no interest in his job / Calls his job "romantic" and asks about it; The job reminds her of their dead son / Sees it as exciting; Their marriage is distant and cold / Her marriage is also distant, which is why he thinks they're alike

Why he keeps telling her stories: he wants her attention and likes feeling important.

His conflict: internal. He's attracted to her and imagines a connection, but he's married and she barely sees him.

Time period: late 20th century, around the 1990s (the book came out in 1999).

His fantasy (p. 55): they'd write letters for years, and he'd be the one who explains India to her. Her interest makes him feel his life and struggles finally make sense.

The Sun Temple: a giant chariot for the sun god Surya, covered in carvings of lovers. It shows the passion missing from both marriages. Looking at the carvings, Mr. Kapasi realizes his own marriage has no real intimacy.

"Walked past her children as if they were strangers" (p. 58): she feels no connection to them.

His wife's silence now "oppresses" him (p. 59): after talking with Mrs. Das, he notices how empty his own marriage is.

Monastic dwellings: small rooms cut into the hills at Udayagiri and Khandagiri where Jain monks lived. Jains follow a religion built on nonviolence.

Why Mrs. Das stays in the car: she says she's tired, but really she wants to talk to Mr. Kapasi alone.

A defile: a narrow pass between hills.

"Not surprising Bobby is brave": a hint that Bobby isn't Mr. Das's son.

"A prickle on his skin": he senses something big and uncomfortable is about to happen: her confession.

Guiding question: the climax and how the characters change

The climax is Mrs. Das's confession and Mr. Kapasi's answer, "Is it really pain you feel, Mrs. Das, or is it guilt?" After it, his fantasy is over and he sees the family clearly. The Dases don't change at all.

What she tells him: Bobby's father is a friend of Mr. Das who stayed with them in New Jersey. Mr. Das and Bobby don't know.

Why she kept it 8 years: guilt and fear of wrecking her family.

Their marriage (tone words): distant, cold, bored, indifferent.

How Raj (Mr. Das) feels: content and clueless. He has no idea anything is wrong.

How Mina (Mrs. Das) feels: trapped and lonely. She married young and feels buried by her kids.

Why Mr. Kapasi is disturbed: she sees him as an older man who can fix her, not as someone she's interested in.

What she wants from him: to "interpret" her pain and make her feel better, like he does for the doctor's patients. She picks him because he's a stranger.

How he interprets her feelings: it's guilt, not pain. He says it because he's hurt and because it's true.

How it resolves his inner conflict: the crush dies. His hope turns into disappointment and he stops caring what she thinks of him.

Why he wants to warn her about the monkeys: the monkeys follow food, and he still feels responsible for the family even after she's hurt him.

The Dases and the monkeys: they can't handle the monkeys, just like they don't really understand India or their own heritage.

The last line: all he keeps of them is a "picture" in his mind, not a friendship. It mirrors Mr. Das's camera.

Happy ending? No. Bobby is safe, but nobody really changes, the secret stays, and Mr. Kapasi loses his hope.

Third level: theme

Abstract words: loneliness, communication, guilt, identity, isolation.

Thematic statement ("This is like life because..."): people can feel most alone inside their closest relationships, and sharing a language with someone doesn't mean they understand you.

Connecting to global issues

Categories: Culture, Identity, and Community (Indian Americans who are tourists in their parents' country), and Beliefs, Values, and Education (what marriage and family should mean).

The tension between Indian heritage and American identity.

The impact of loneliness on marriage and family.

Communication as a source of misunderstanding.

Local example: families in NYC who visit a parents' home country and feel like outsiders there.

How to answer a question

1. Claim: name the element or technique and say what it does. 2. Evidence: a short quote or a specific moment. 3. Explain: how that moment shows it. 4. Link: what it adds to the theme.

Example: Lahiri uses situational irony to show that real understanding is about more than language. Mr. Kapasi is a professional interpreter, yet when Mrs. Das confesses her secret, he asks, "Is it really pain you feel, Mrs. Das, or is it guilt?" and she walks away. The man paid to understand others can't understand the one person who asks him to, which shows that sharing a language doesn't mean people truly connect.
